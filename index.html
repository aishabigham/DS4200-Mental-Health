How do daily habits shape mental health?

We explore how everyday lifestyle behaviors — sleep, exercise, diet, and screen time — relate to stress levels, happiness, and mental health conditions in a global survey of 3,000 individuals. As students ourselves, we are particularly interested in patterns among young adults and what lifestyle choices might support better mental well-being.

Dataset

About the data

Our dataset was sourced from Kaggle and is a collection of global survey responses related to lifestyle habits and mental well-being. It contains 3,000 observations and 12 variables, with each row representing one individual's survey response. The data draws from international surveys and research studies examining factors associated with mental health, daily behaviors, and overall well-being. Since the dataset is observational, we focus on identifying patterns and correlations rather than establishing causation.

Visualization 1 — D3.js
Frequency of mental health conditions

A bar chart showing the count of each reported mental health condition across all 3,000 respondents. This overview establishes the landscape of mental health in our dataset before exploring lifestyle correlations.

ℹ️ How to interact: Hover over any bar to see the exact count and percentage of respondents with that condition. The hovered bar will dim to highlight your selection.
Takeaway: Mental health conditions are distributed nearly equally across the four categories — Anxiety (628), PTSD (624), Depression (580), and Bipolar Disorder (573). Anxiety is the most frequently reported condition. The even distribution suggests this dataset captures a broad cross-section of mental health experiences rather than skewing toward one diagnosis, making it a well-balanced foundation for further analysis.
Visualization 2 — Matplotlib
Sleep hours by diet type

Box plots showing the distribution of nightly sleep hours across five dietary patterns: Balanced, Junk Food, Keto, Vegan, and Vegetarian. This examines whether dietary habits are reflected in sleep duration.

Takeaway: Sleep distributions are broadly similar across all five diet types, with median sleep hours ranging narrowly between 6.3 and 6.6 hours. Junk Food and Vegetarian diets show the highest medians, while Balanced diets show the lowest — a counterintuitive result. The substantial overlap across all groups suggests that diet type alone is not a strong predictor of sleep duration.
Visualization 3 — Matplotlib
Sleep hours by happiness level

Box plots comparing sleep hours across three happiness level categories — Low (score 0–4), Medium (4–7), and High (7–10). This examines whether happier individuals tend to sleep more.

Takeaway: Individuals with high happiness scores show a modestly higher median sleep duration compared to those with low happiness. However, the distributions overlap substantially across all three groups, indicating that sleep alone does not determine happiness — it plays a supporting role alongside other lifestyle factors.
Visualization 4 — Altair
Happiness, stress & exercise — interactive dashboard

A three-panel Altair dashboard combining: happiness by stress level, exercise by happiness faceted by stress, and stress by happiness faceted by exercise. A shared dropdown filter links all three panels.

ℹ️ How to interact: Use the dropdown menu at the top of the chart to filter all three panels by stress level (Low, Moderate, or High). This lets you compare exercise–happiness patterns within each stress group simultaneously.
Takeaway: Median happiness scores are strikingly similar across all three stress levels, suggesting stress alone does not strongly predict happiness. In contrast, exercise level shows a clearer positive relationship with happiness across all stress conditions — higher exercise consistently corresponds to higher happiness scores regardless of stress.
Visualization 5 — Altair
Mental health conditions — sleep, screen time & happiness by gender

A linked dashboard where clicking a mental health condition filters three histograms showing sleep patterns, screen time behavior, and happiness distribution — each broken down by gender.

ℹ️ How to interact: Click any bar in the top chart to select a mental health condition. The three charts below will update to show sleep, screen time, and happiness distributions for only that condition, split by gender.
Takeaway: Sleep patterns are broadly consistent across all mental health conditions and genders, suggesting sleep alone does not differentiate conditions. Screen time shows more variation and clusters at higher ranges (5–8 hours/day) across conditions, indicating excessive screen time may be associated with poorer mental health outcomes. Happiness distributions are similar across conditions but show moderate spread, suggesting it interacts with other lifestyle factors rather than acting as a standalone predictor.

A heatmap comparing social interaction scores against happiness scores, faceted by gender and filterable by age group. Darker cells indicate higher density of respondents at that combination.

Visualization 7 — Altair
Social interaction vs happiness — filtered by mental health condition

The strongest pattern in our dataset. A heatmap of social interaction score vs happiness score, filterable by mental health condition. Higher social interaction consistently maps to higher happiness across all conditions.

ℹ️ How to interact: Use the dropdown to switch between Anxiety, Depression, Bipolar, and PTSD. Observe how the density pattern shifts — especially how PTSD shows the strongest improvement in happiness with increased social interaction.
Takeaway: This is the strongest pattern in our analysis. Social interaction has a clear positive relationship with happiness across all mental health conditions. Individuals with PTSD show the most pronounced improvement in happiness with higher social interaction. Those with anxiety and bipolar disorder show weaker but still positive patterns. This suggests social engagement is a meaningful lever for well-being, with its impact varying by condition.
Visualization 8 — Altair
Average happiness heatmap — stress × exercise level

A heatmap showing average happiness at every combination of stress level and exercise level. Color intensity (viridis scale) encodes the average happiness value, making interaction effects easy to spot at a glance.

ℹ️ How to interact: Hover over any cell to see the exact average happiness score for that stress–exercise combination.
Takeaway: Higher exercise levels are associated with higher average happiness across all stress levels. The combination of low exercise and low stress yields a surprisingly low average happiness — suggesting physical activity is independently important for well-being, not merely a stress buffer. For high-stress individuals, increasing exercise appears to partially offset stress's negative effect on happiness.

A violin/density plot showing the full distribution shape of happiness scores for each stress level, filterable by exercise level. Unlike box plots, this reveals the entire shape and spread of the distribution.

Conclusion

What we learned

Our analysis of 3,000 global survey responses reveals two consistent and meaningful findings: exercise and social interaction are the strongest lifestyle predictors of happiness, regardless of mental health condition, stress level, or demographic group.

Exercise & social interaction drive happiness

Higher exercise levels and greater social interaction both consistently correspond to higher happiness scores across all conditions and demographics. These are the two most actionable lifestyle factors for improving well-being.

Social interaction's impact varies by condition

There is a consistent positive relationship between social interaction and happiness across all mental health conditions, but the strength varies. Individuals with PTSD show the strongest improvement in happiness with increased social interaction, while those with anxiety and bipolar disorder exhibit weaker or more inconsistent patterns.

Stress level alone doesn't determine happiness

Average happiness differed by less than 0.5 points across low, moderate, and high stress groups. How people manage stress — through exercise and social engagement — matters more than stress level itself.

Sleep and diet show modest associations

Higher happiness is associated with slightly more sleep, but the effect is small. Diet type shows minimal differences in sleep hours. These factors play a supporting role rather than a primary one.

Future directions

Since this dataset is observational, we cannot establish causation. Future work could use longitudinal data to track how changes in exercise and social habits correspond to changes in well-being over time. It would also be valuable to include more granular measures — such as exercise type and intensity, social interaction quality, and sleep quality rather than just duration. Interventional studies targeting both exercise and social engagement as low-cost, accessible mental health support strategies would be a particularly impactful next step.
